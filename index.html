Hemi Hand: a hand orthosis for spastic hemiparesis

A patent-pending, body-worn device designed around the person who uses it.

People with spastic hemiparesis often struggle to open the affected hand. Our team of five Bachelor of Engineering candidates designed the Hemi Hand so the wearer can put it on easily and use it to expand the hand.

A patient who was also the project's sponsor guided the team, and we consulted occupational therapists and physicians to inform the design. The device went through more than 500 iterations, from whole-architecture changes down to small fit details, starting in foamcore, inflatables, and other soft materials before moving to 3D-printed parts.

The final device is modular, built from a cone, body, base, top, thumb support, and a set of wedges (Fig. 1).

We designed the device over two semesters of dedicated course time. Since then I have kept refining it, helped build Hemi Hand into a non-profit, and mentored a second student team that added patentable components, which are not shown here.

Clip fixture for static conductivity testing

Making conductivity readings on a carbon-loaded polymer repeatable.

The lab was characterizing ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) mixed with 0 to 10 wt% conductive carbon black, a candidate material for an implantable piezoresistive strain sensor. This was a two-term first-year research project that also included Instron tensile testing and SEM analysis.

Problem

The original setup held alligator clips on a metal block with double-sided tape. Torque from the clips made them shift over time, including across the sample itself, until they fell off. Contact spacing and pressure changed between readings, so the data were inconsistent.

Solution

I designed a printed fixture that holds each clip under tension at a set angle and a fixed distance apart, using as little material as possible. The first iterations fixed the measurement; the later ones made the fixture faster to set up and put away. I presented this work as a poster at the Wetterhahn Science Symposium.

Measuring conductivity while a sample stretches

A custom rig and method for reading conductivity during tensile testing.

A piezoresistive strain sensor has to report conductivity while it deforms, so the static fixture led to a harder question: how does conductivity change during tensile testing? A PhD student had built a setup to measure this, but it did not work (Fig. 4).

I designed the rig and the test method from scratch and built every piece myself, bandsawing and assembling the wooden structure around printed clip mounts. The rig sits on an Instron tensile tester and keeps electrical contact on the sample while it stretches to roughly 300% of its original length. It took 15 iterations to get a setup that worked reliably.

The method may support a future lab publication, so its details are kept brief here. I'm happy to walk through it in conversation.

Hardware for a glenoid loosening simulator

Two machined parts that let a shoulder simulator produce clinically relevant results.

Titanium offset couplers

The lab needed to compare how a shoulder implant loosens when the joint is loose versus properly tensioned. I designed a set of titanium couplers with different offsets, each setting a different subluxation translation distance, so the team could study both the rate and the shape (for example, the linearity) of loosening. I turned and milled them myself.

Aluminum load bracer

The ASTM standard for this test calls for a 750 N joint force. I designed and milled an aluminum bracer, braced against the back wall of the simulator, that holds that load steady for the whole test. One test ran for 27.56 hours.

Both parts worked on the first iteration.

Published. Will DV, Solberg PC, Bell JE, Landi GM, Van Citters DW. The Importance of Off-Axis Loading and Articulation in Loosening of Cemented Glenoids. Biomechanics. 2026;6(3):70. doi:10.3390/biomechanics6030070

Single-surgeon diaphyseal fracture reduction tool

A tool that lets an orthopedic surgeon reduce a long-bone fracture without an assistant.

Reducing a diaphyseal fracture usually takes more than one pair of hands. Our tool lets a surgeon do it alone and aims to make alignment easier and more consistent. We split the device by subsystem. I contributed to ideation across the whole tool and did the CAD design of the screw that anchors it in the bone, taking it through 10 iterations. Teammates designed the tap screw and the handle.

Screw design

  • Hollow core so the reduction tool can pass through it.
  • Interfaces for both the tap wrench and the surgeon's handle.
  • Triple-start buttress thread, 1 mm thick, so the screw advances into the medullary canal evenly and holds against up to 400 N.

Lattice scaffolds for bone regeneration

Printable bioglass scaffolds tuned for cell growth and mechanical strength.

As a Fulbright researcher in Erlangen, Germany, working with FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg and University Hospital Regensburg, I designed bone scaffolds in nTopology and printed them on a Prusa SL1S Speed using a resin and bioglass slurry. Over 100+ iterations I varied porosity, wall thickness, and overall dimensions.

Lattice selection

I explored many lattice types, including lidinoid, primitive, and Cartesian-oriented designs, before choosing gyroid and diamond TPMS structures for the study. I show Voronoi designs here as well because they take a different, biomimetic approach.

Voronoi. Tessellations with 500 to 1,000 seed points and a range of wall thicknesses, all held at 75% porosity to isolate the effect of structure.

TPMS. Triply periodic minimal surfaces maximize surface area relative to volume, which supports cell adhesion and growth. I compared gyroid cells, expected to offer more surface area and better cell growth, with diamond cells, expected to be stronger. Pores ranged from 20 to 800 µm to allow cell infiltration and blood vessel formation.
